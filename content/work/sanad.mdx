## The Tension That Shapes Everything

New businesses signing up for a fintech product have to prove they're real before they can move money. The process is called KYB, Know Your Business. It's slow, document-heavy, and legally mandatory. So businesses start it, hit friction, and abandon, and the company loses a customer it already paid to acquire at the worst possible moment.

You cannot delete steps. Regulators require this verification, so "reduce friction" in the usual sense is off the table. The real design problem is narrower and harder:

> How do you make a legally rigid, unavoidable process feel fast, clear, and trustworthy, without weakening what it's legally required to do?

That reframe is the whole project. It rules out the easy answer (fewer fields) and forces every decision to earn its place against a constraint I couldn't move. I designed the full flow for a fictional business-banking product, Sanad, and built it as a working React prototype. It's below. Click through it.

## Who I Designed For

A founder or ops lead completing verification for their company. Busy. Not a compliance expert. Often missing at least one document. And mildly distrustful, because they're handing sensitive company and personal data to a product they met ten minutes ago.

So I designed for someone impatient, under-informed, and a little guarded, not a patient user who reads instructions.

## Scope

The verification journey only, six screens: onboarding overview, business details, beneficial owners, document upload, verification-pending, and rejection with resubmit. Deliberately out of scope: the banking product itself, back-office review tooling, and pricing. I wanted depth on the journey, not breadth across a product.

---

## Research

I ran focused desk research on how three leaders handle business onboarding: **Stripe**, **Mercury**, and **Airwallex**, through their docs, help centers, and an annotated teardown of Mercury's live flow. Three findings shaped the design.

**The best pattern I saw.** Mercury splits onboarding into two phases: create the account with light info first, then do the heavy KYB. It builds commitment before asking for documents, and it means a drop-off isn't a dead loss, since there's enough info to re-engage the user.

**The worst pattern.** Across all three, the waiting state after submission is thin. Users are told review takes days, then handed a vague "we'll be in touch." The moment that most needs reassurance gets the least design.

**The gap nobody fills.** The emotional design of rejection. Every product documents what to resubmit. None make a rejected document feel non-accusatory. That gap became the screen I invested the most in.

## The Design Decisions

I documented each key decision the same way: problem, constraint, the options I weighed, and what I chose. The rejected option stays visible, because what you decide not to do says as much as what you ship.

### 1. Show the whole path up front

**Problem.** An anxious, busy user needs to know what they're in for before they'll commit.

**Constraint.** The process genuinely is long. I can't pretend it's two fields.

**Options.** (a) Reveal steps one at a time to make it feel shorter. (b) Show the entire map, every step with time estimates, before they start.

**Choice.** The full map. I rejected (a) because hidden scope doesn't reduce drop-off, it delays it to the moment the user feels misled. Honesty about length ("four steps, about 10 minutes, then 1 to 2 days to verify") is what earns the first click.

### 2. Explain why at every sensitive request

**Problem.** Non-experts abandon when asked for something invasive with no reason given.

**Constraint.** Some requests genuinely are invasive (registration numbers, owner IDs) and can't be removed.

**Options.** (a) A generic "we take privacy seriously" reassurance up top. (b) An inline, per-field "why we ask" tied to the specific request.

**Choice.** Inline and specific. I rejected (a) because blanket reassurance is wallpaper; users don't read it. A one-line reason next to the scary field ("this confirms your company legally exists") converts anxiety into trust exactly where the anxiety occurs.

### 3. Build momentum before the heavy lifting

**Problem.** Confidence early keeps people moving into the hard parts.

**Constraint.** The hard parts, documents and multiple owners, are unavoidable.

**Options.** (a) Ask for everything in one long, honest form. (b) Sequence easy-to-hard, pre-filling and confirming what's already known.

**Choice.** Sequenced, with pre-filled fields the user just confirms, borrowed from Mercury. I rejected (a) because front-loading effort before any sense of progress is the fastest way to lose someone at step one.

### 4. Never hard-block a willing user

**Problem.** The most common real-world drop-off cause is "I don't have that document right now."

**Constraint.** The document is still required. I can't waive it.

**Options.** (a) Block progress until every required file is uploaded. (b) Let the user mark a document "add later," keep moving, and save automatically.

**Choice.** The escape hatch. I rejected (a) because a hard block turns a temporary problem (missing a file today) into a permanent one (never comes back). Required doesn't mean required this second. Save-and-resume keeps a willing user from becoming a lost one.

### 5. Make the wait honest instead of empty

**Problem.** Verification takes days on the backend, and a silent "pending" screen erodes the trust the flow just built.

**Constraint.** I can't make the review faster. That's a compliance-team reality.

**Options.** (a) A spinner and "we'll be in touch." (b) An honest status: a real timeline, plain-language "what's happening," what they can do meanwhile, and how they'll be told.

**Choice.** The honest status. This is the worst pattern from research turned into an opportunity. I rejected (a) because false brevity ("almost done!") breaks trust the moment it turns out to be untrue. Transparency about a slow step beats a cheerful lie about a fast one.

### 6. Handle rejection with dignity

**Problem.** A document gets rejected. Told badly, the user feels accused and quits at the finish line.

**Constraint.** I have to be specific about the failure. Vagueness helps no one fix it.

**Options.** (a) A generic "verification failed, please resubmit." (b) Name the exact item and reason, reassure that everything else is saved, and drop them straight to fixing just that one thing.

**Choice.** Specific, blameless, one tap forward. This is the gap from research, and the screen I'm proudest of. I rejected (a) because "verification failed" reads as an accusation and a dead end. "Your proof of address is a little out of date, everything else is verified" is the same information delivered as a small fixable task instead of a verdict.

---

## Two Threads That Tie It Together

Every screen is really an expression of two principles:

1. **Explain why at every sensitive moment.** Trust is built request by request, not with a policy page.
2. **Never hard-block a willing user.** Momentum is the scarcest resource in onboarding. Protect it everywhere.

---

## What I'd Test Next

Because this is a concept, I have design intent, not proof, and I'd rather be honest about that than invent a "40% lift." If I took this further I'd test three things: whether the "add later" escape hatch actually recovers droppers or just defers them, whether the per-field "why we ask" reduces abandonment on the owners step specifically, and whether the honest waiting screen lowers support tickets during the review window.

Those are the hypotheses the design is built to test.

> Regulators decide what the steps are. Design decides how they feel. That gap is the whole job.
