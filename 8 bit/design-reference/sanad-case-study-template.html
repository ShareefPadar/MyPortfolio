03THE PROBLEM

New businesses signing up for a fintech product have to prove they're real before they can move money. The process is called KYB — Know Your Business. It's slow, document-heavy and legally mandatory. So businesses start it, hit friction, and give up. The company loses a customer it already paid to acquire, at the worst possible moment.

I know this one firsthand. Going through Companies House verification in the UK, I didn't know how many steps were coming. I was missing one document. There was no way to save and come back, so stopping halfway meant starting over. The verification itself couldn't change. The experience of it clearly could.

The tension that shapes everything

You cannot delete steps. Regulators require this verification, so "reduce friction" in the usual sense is off the table. The real problem is narrower and harder.

THE REFRAME

How do you make a legally rigid, unavoidable process feel fast, clear and trustworthy — without weakening what it's legally required to do?

Designed for
A founder or ops lead completing verification for their company. Busy, not a compliance expert, often missing a document, and a little distrustful — they're handing sensitive data to a product they met ten minutes ago.
Scope
The verification journey only. Six screens, from overview through to rejection and resubmit.
Out of scope
The banking product itself, back-office review tooling, pricing. Depth on the journey, not breadth across a product.
04RESEARCH

The waiting screen and the rejection screen are where everyone gives up

I ran focused desk research on how Stripe, Mercury and Airwallex handle business onboarding — docs, help centres, and an annotated teardown of Mercury's live flow. Three findings shaped the design.

BEST PATTERN

Mercury splits onboarding in two: light account creation first, heavy KYB after. Commitment builds before documents are asked for.

WORST PATTERN

All three ship a thin waiting state. The moment that most needs reassurance gets the least design.

THE GAP

Nobody makes a rejected document feel non-accusatory. That gap became the screen I put the most into.

05THE DESIGN DECISIONS

I worked through each decision the same way — the problem, the options I weighed, what I chose. Where it matters I've kept the rejected option visible, because what you decide not to do says as much as what you ship.

01

Show the whole path up front

An anxious, busy user needs to know what they're in for before they'll commit — and the process genuinely is long. Reveal steps one at a time to make it feel shorter, or show the entire map with time estimates before they start?

WHAT I CHOSE

The full map. Revealing it piece by piece doesn't reduce drop-off, it delays it to the moment the user feels misled. Being honest about the length up front is what earns the first click.

04

Never hard-block a willing user

The most common real-world reason people drop off is "I don't have that document right now." The document is still required — I can't waive it. Block progress until every file is uploaded, or let the user mark one "add later" and keep moving?

WHAT I CHOSE

The escape hatch. A hard block turns a temporary problem into a permanent one. Required doesn't mean required this second. This is the exact wall I hit at Companies House, so it wasn't hypothetical to me.

06

Handle rejection with dignity

A document gets rejected. Told badly, the user feels accused and quits at the finish line — but vagueness helps nobody fix it. Generic "verification failed", or name the exact item and drop them straight to fixing it?

WHAT I CHOSE

Specific, blameless, one tap forward. "Verification failed" reads as an accusation and a dead end. "Your proof of address is a little out of date, everything else is verified" is the same fact delivered as a fixable task instead of a verdict.

06AUDITING MY OWN BUILD

The running code failed in four places a screenshot would have hidden

Because the prototype is real working code, I could hold it to the same standard as the writing. After the first build I audited it against best practice. It failed in four places, and the failures are worth showing.

The consent checkbox was a dark pattern

V1 had the "I confirm this is accurate" box pre-checked and non-interactive. Legal consent has to be an affirmative act, so the fix was a real checkbox with Submit disabled until ticked.

The build contradicted my own Decision 4

The "add later" escape hatch only existed on the optional document, where it does nothing. I extended it to required documents, which was the entire point.

"Saved automatically" was a lie

The label was on screen, but going back wiped uploads and owners. I moved state up so the promise the UI makes is one the prototype actually keeps.

A date that didn't add up

The rejected document was named as a June bill while the reason said "issued in January". A compliance reviewer would catch that in seconds, so I should too — fixed alongside a set of accessibility gaps.

None of these showed up in a screenshot. They only surfaced because the thing runs — which is the whole argument for designing in code. It makes your claims testable, including against yourself.

07TRY THE FLOW

Built in a Mercury-inspired system on purpose — lavender canvas, white cards, indigo accents, all pulled from the desk research. Working inside an established fintech language is closer to the actual job than inventing a novel one.

What I'd do next

This is a concept, so what I have is design intent, not proof. I'd rather say that plainly than invent a "40% lift." If I took it further there are three things I'd test: whether the "add later" hatch recovers droppers or just defers them, whether the per-field "why we ask" cuts abandonment on the owners step, and whether the honest waiting screen lowers support tickets during review.

REGULATORS DECIDE WHAT THE STEPS ARE. DESIGN DECIDES HOW THEY FEEL.
